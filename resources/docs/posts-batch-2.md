### In brief

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a lifelong condition that affects movement and posture. It is caused by an injury to, or a difference in, the developing brain. The brain injury does not get worse, but a child's needs change as they grow. Regular, goal-based rehab, planned with parents, helps many children do more for themselves. At Rehabify in Bengaluru, Dr. Dharshan N A, a physiatrist, answers the questions families ask most in the first months after hearing the words "cerebral palsy".

### What is cerebral palsy, in simple words?

The parts of the brain that control movement send messages to the muscles. In CP, some of those messages do not get through as they should. This can make muscles stiff, floppy or hard to control, and it can affect balance and coordination.

CP looks different in every child. Some children walk on their own with a slight limp. Others use a walker or a wheelchair. It may affect one side of the body, mostly the legs, or the whole body. Doctors describe the type by how the muscles behave:

- **Spastic CP,** the most common type, with stiff, tight muscles
- **Dyskinetic CP,** with movements the child cannot control
- **Ataxic CP,** with shaky movements and poor balance
- **Mixed CP,** with features of more than one type

### What caused it, and could we have stopped it?

This is often the first question parents ask, and many carry guilt for a long time. Common causes include being born very early, low oxygen around birth, infection during pregnancy or in early life, bleeding in the brain, or jaundice that became very severe. In many children, no single cause is ever found.

In most cases, nothing the parents did or did not do caused CP. If you have questions about the birth, ask your child's doctor to go through the records with you. It is better to talk about it than to carry it alone.

### Will my child walk and talk?

This is the hardest question to answer early, and an honest doctor will not guess too soon. By around age 2, the pattern of movement becomes clearer. Doctors then often use a scale called the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS), with levels I to V, to describe how a child moves and to plan care.

Speech depends on how CP affects the muscles of the mouth and on hearing, learning and other factors. A speech and language therapist can assess this early. Children who find speech hard can still communicate well with signs, picture boards or communication devices.

The goal of rehab is not only walking. It is moving around, playing, learning and taking part in family life in whatever way suits your child.

### Will it get worse?

The brain injury does not get worse. But as children grow, bones can grow faster than tight muscles can stretch. This can lead to tighter joints, a change in posture or walking, hip problems and pain. That is why regular reviews matter most during growth spurts.

Hip checks are an important part of care. Children with CP who do not walk much are at higher risk of the hip slowly moving out of its socket, often without pain at first. Guidelines advise regular hip X-rays for children at risk.

### What treatment helps?

There is no treatment that removes CP, so be careful of anyone who promises one. What helps is a steady plan with clear goals:

- **Physiotherapy** for strength, balance, sitting, standing and walking
- **Occupational therapy** for hand use, dressing, feeding and play
- **Speech and language therapy** for communication and safe swallowing
- **A home programme** built into daily routines such as bath time and play
- **Splints and orthoses** to hold joints in a better position and help with standing or walking
- **Seating, standing frames and mobility aids** chosen for your child's size and goals
- **Spasticity care,** including medicines or targeted injections into tight muscles when suitable, always combined with therapy
- **Referral to an orthopaedic surgeon** if tight muscles or joints need an operation

Short, regular practice at home usually counts for more than long sessions once a week.

### What else should we watch for?

CP can come with other needs that other specialists also look after. Mention any of these at your child's review:

- Feeding difficulty, coughing or choking with meals, or poor weight gain
- Seizures
- Vision or hearing problems
- Constipation, drooling or poor sleep
- Pain, which children may show through crying, poor sleep or changes in behaviour
- Learning or attention difficulties

### How a physiatrist helps

A physiatrist looks at the whole child: muscle tone, joints, posture, walking, hand use, pain and daily life. At Rehabify, Dr. Dharshan N A examines your child through play, sets goals with you, and writes one plan that your child's therapists and school can follow. He refers you to a neurologist, orthopaedic surgeon or other specialist when needed. Follow-ups can be at the clinic or by video consult.

### Looking after yourself

Caring for a child with CP goes on for years. Ask for help, share tasks within the family, and connect with other parents if you can. Your health and rest matter for your child too.

### When to get help urgently

Callout:

> Call 108 or 112 or go to an emergency department if your child has a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, trouble breathing, choking while eating, or is very drowsy and hard to wake.

### FAQs

1. **How early can cerebral palsy be diagnosed?** Signs can sometimes be seen in the first months, especially in babies born early or after a difficult birth. Some children are diagnosed later, around age 1 to 2, when delays in sitting or walking become clear. If you are worried, ask for a check early rather than waiting, because therapy can start before a firm diagnosis.
2. **Is cerebral palsy inherited?** Usually not. Most CP is caused by an injury to the developing brain, not by a gene passed from parent to child. In a small number of children a genetic cause is found. If CP runs in your family or no cause is clear, your doctor may suggest genetic tests or a referral to a genetics specialist.
3. **How often should my child be reviewed?** It depends on age, type of CP and how fast your child is growing. Many children are reviewed every few months, and more often during growth spurts or after a new treatment such as splints or injections. Hip X-rays follow their own schedule, which your doctor will explain.
4. **Can therapy be done at home?** Much of it can. Your therapist will teach you short activities that fit into daily life, such as stretches at bath time or standing practice during play. Clinic sessions are still needed to check progress, update goals and adjust splints. Video consults can help with follow-ups between clinic visits.
5. **Will my child need surgery?** Many children do not. Some develop tight muscles, hip problems or foot deformities that do not respond to stretching, splints or injections. In that case, the doctor will explain why and refer you to a paediatric orthopaedic surgeon. Rehab before and after surgery is an important part of the plan.

### In brief

Many toddlers walk on their toes now and then while they learn to walk, and it often settles by about age 2 to 3. Toe walking should be checked if it continues after that age, affects one leg only, started after your child had been walking flat-footed, or comes with stiffness, falls or delays in other skills. At Rehabify in Bengaluru, Dr. Dharshan N A, a physiatrist, checks children who walk on their toes and helps families decide whether to watch, stretch or treat.

### Why young children walk on their toes

When children first start walking, their balance and muscle control are still developing. Walking on tiptoe now and then is a normal part of learning. Most children slowly move to a heel-to-toe walk as they get steadier.

Some children carry on walking on their toes after age 2 or 3 even though their muscles, nerves and development are otherwise normal. Doctors call this idiopathic toe walking, which means no cause is found. It often runs in families and many of these children improve over time. But toe walking can also be an early sign of another condition, so it is worth checking when it does not settle.

### When it is usually not a worry

Toe walking is less likely to be a concern if your child:

- Is under 2 and still new to walking
- Toe walks only sometimes, for example on cold floors or when excited
- Can stand and walk flat-footed when asked
- Is meeting other milestones, such as running, climbing and using their hands
- Has no stiffness, pain or frequent falls

### When to get it checked

Book a check if your child:

- Still walks on their toes most of the time after age 2 to 3
- Toe walks on one side only
- Cannot put their heels down when standing, even when asked
- Started toe walking after already walking flat-footed
- Falls often, seems clumsy, or tires more than other children
- Has stiff or very floppy legs, or uses one hand much more than the other
- Is late with sitting, walking, speech or play
- Has trouble climbing stairs or getting up from the floor
- Has back pain, leg weakness or changes in bladder or bowel control

### What can be behind toe walking

- **Tight calf muscles or Achilles tendon,** present from birth or developing over time
- **Mild cerebral palsy,** which can show as toe walking, often more on one side
- **Muscular dystrophy,** especially in boys who also have large calves, falls and trouble with stairs
- **Autism and sensory differences,** where toe walking is common
- **Problems in the spine or nerves,** which are less common but important to find
- **Idiopathic toe walking,** where no cause is found after a full check

### What happens at the check

1. **Your story.** When your child started walking, when toe walking began, family history, birth history and other milestones.
2. **Watching your child move.** Walking, running, squatting, standing on heels and getting up from the floor, done as play.
3. **Examination.** How far the ankles bend, muscle tone, strength, reflexes, the spine and the hips.
4. **Tests if needed.** Most children do not need any. Blood tests, nerve tests or scans are used only when the examination points to a cause.
5. **A clear plan,** explained in plain words.

Short videos of your child walking at home, barefoot and in shoes, are very helpful.

### What helps

Treatment depends on the cause, your child's age and how tight the calves are:

- **Watching and reviewing** for younger children whose ankles still move well
- **Daily calf stretches** shown by a physiotherapist, done at home as part of routine
- **Activities** that use the heels and calves, such as walking uphill, heel walking games and squatting play
- **Supportive shoes or orthoses** (ankle foot orthoses) to hold the foot in a better position
- **Serial casting,** where a series of plaster casts gently stretches tight calves over a few weeks
- **Targeted injections** into tight calf muscles in some children, always combined with stretching and therapy
- **Referral to an orthopaedic surgeon** for lengthening of the calf or tendon, if the tightness is fixed and other steps have not helped

If another condition is found, such as cerebral palsy or muscular dystrophy, care is planned for that condition too.

### What you can do now

- Encourage barefoot play on safe surfaces, such as grass or sand
- Make stretching part of a daily routine, such as after a bath
- Take short videos every few weeks so you can see changes over time
- Do not force your child to walk flat-footed or scold them for toe walking

### How Rehabify helps

Dr. Dharshan N A examines your child through play, looks for any cause behind the toe walking, and plans stretching, orthoses or casting with your child's physiotherapist. When needed, he refers you to a paediatric neurologist, developmental paediatrician or orthopaedic surgeon. Follow-ups can be at the clinic or by video consult.

### When to get help urgently

Callout:

> See a doctor within days if toe walking starts suddenly with back pain, leg weakness, or new problems with bladder or bowel control. Call 108 or 112 or go to an emergency department if your child has sudden weakness, a seizure, a fall with a head injury, or is very drowsy and hard to wake.

### FAQs

1. **At what age should toe walking stop?** Most children walk with a heel-to-toe pattern by about age 2 to 3. Occasional toe walking after that can still be normal, especially when excited. If your child toe walks most of the time after age 3, or cannot put their heels down when asked, it is worth a check with a doctor.
2. **Is toe walking a sign of autism?** It can be. Toe walking is more common in autistic children, often linked to sensory differences. But most children who toe walk are not autistic. If you also notice delays in speech, play or social skills, mention them at the check, and the doctor can refer you to a specialist who assesses autism.
3. **Will my child grow out of toe walking?** Many children do, especially when no cause is found and the ankles still move well. Some keep toe walking and develop tight calves, which can make walking and running harder. Regular reviews help catch tightness early, when stretching and orthoses are more likely to help.
4. **Do special shoes help?** Supportive shoes alone rarely stop toe walking. Ankle foot orthoses, which are custom-made splints worn inside shoes, can hold the foot in a better position and stretch the calf. The doctor will advise if your child needs them and check the fit as your child grows.
5. **Is surgery needed for toe walking?** Rarely. Surgery to lengthen the calf muscle or Achilles tendon is considered only when tightness is fixed and has not improved with stretching, orthoses or casting. If this applies to your child, the doctor will explain why and refer you to a paediatric orthopaedic surgeon.

### In brief

Children with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) can go to school, learn, play and make friends. Because muscles get weaker over time, school needs some planning, and that plan needs to change as your child grows. Therapy helps keep joints flexible, keeps your child active in a safe way and supports independence. It does not stop the condition. At Rehabify in Bengaluru, Dr. Dharshan N A, a physiatrist, helps families plan therapy and school support alongside the child's neurologist.

### Why planning matters

DMD affects the muscles used for walking, climbing stairs, getting up from the floor and, later, lifting the arms. Children with DMD tire more easily than their classmates. Small changes at school can save energy for learning and play, and lower the chance of falls.

Plan before problems start. A meeting with the class teacher and school head at the start of each school year, or when something changes, works well for many families.

### Getting around school

- **Classroom on the ground floor,** or close to a lift, so your child avoids stairs
- **Short routes** between the classroom, toilet, lunch area and pickup point
- **Extra time** to move between rooms, with a friend to walk alongside
- **A second set of books** at home so the school bag stays light
- **Safe floors,** with care on wet or crowded corridors where falls are more likely
- **A wheelchair or scooter for long distances,** such as school trips or sports day, even while your child still walks in class

### Seating, toilets and rest

- **A chair at the right height** so feet rest flat and your child can stand up without strain
- **An accessible toilet** close to the classroom, with grab rails, and permission to go when needed
- **Rest breaks** during the day, and a quiet place to sit
- **Help with heavy tasks,** such as carrying trays or moving furniture

Some children avoid drinking water at school so they do not need the toilet. Watch for this, as it can cause constipation and other problems.

### PE and play

Children with DMD should stay active, but the type of activity matters.

- **Usually encouraged:** swimming, gentle cycling, walking, and play at your child's own pace
- **Usually avoided:** repeated jumping, running downhill, trampolines, heavy lifting and any exercise that strains the muscles
- **Stop when tired.** Your child should be allowed to sit out without being singled out
- **Warning signs of too much:** muscle pain or extreme tiredness the next day, or dark urine after exercise

Ask the PE teacher to give your child a role in team games, such as keeping score or coaching, when running is too hard.

### Learning and attention

Some children with DMD have difficulty with reading, memory, attention or speech. This is part of the condition for some boys, not a sign of laziness. If you or the teacher notice problems, ask for an assessment by a psychologist or developmental paediatrician. Support such as extra time, short instructions and a seat near the teacher can help.

### Writing and exams

As the arms and hands tire, writing can become slow. Ask about:

- Extra time in exams and class tests
- Using a laptop or tablet
- A scribe or reader where allowed
- Copies of notes instead of copying from the board

Most exam boards in India offer concessions for students with disabilities. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 supports inclusive education and reasonable adjustments at school. Ask the school how to apply, and keep copies of medical letters.

### Therapy outside school

- **Daily stretching** of the calves, hips and knees, usually done by a parent at home
- **Night splints** to hold the ankles in a stretched position during sleep
- **Safe, regular activity,** such as swimming
- **Standing and positioning** to protect joints and posture
- **Equipment at the right time,** such as a lightweight manual wheelchair and, later, a powered wheelchair
- **Regular checks** of the heart, breathing, spine and bones, arranged with your child's neurologist

Many children with DMD take steroid medicines prescribed by their neurologist. These can affect weight, mood and bone strength, so your rehab plan takes them into account.

### Talking with your child

Children notice when they cannot keep up. Talk openly, using words that suit their age. With your child's agreement, some families help the teacher explain to classmates why your child uses a chair or sits out of some games. Being part of the group matters as much as any piece of equipment.

### How Rehabify helps

Dr. Dharshan N A checks strength, joint range, posture and walking, plans stretching and splints, and advises on equipment and safe activity. With your consent, he writes a letter for the school with suggested changes. He works with your child's neurologist and refers for heart, breathing or spine checks when needed. Follow-ups can be at the clinic or by video consult.

### When to get help urgently

Callout:

> Call 108 or 112 or go to an emergency department if your child has trouble breathing, a weak cough with a chest infection, chest pain, fainting, or a fall with severe pain or a possible broken bone. After a fall or fracture, sudden breathlessness, confusion or drowsiness is an emergency. If your child takes steroid medicines and is vomiting or very unwell, seek urgent care and tell the team about the steroids.

### FAQs

1. **Should my child go to a regular school?** Many children with DMD attend mainstream schools with the right support, such as ground floor classrooms, rest breaks and exam help. Some families choose a special school or a mix of both. The right choice depends on your child, the school's facilities and how willing the school is to plan with you. The doctor can help you think it through.
2. **Can my child take part in sports day?** Often yes, with changes. Your child might join shorter events, take part in a wheelchair, or help with scoring and announcements. Avoid events with repeated jumping or straining. Talk to the PE teacher well before the day so your child is included rather than left out.
3. **How much stretching is needed?** Most children need daily stretches of the calves, hips and knees, held gently for a slow count. Your physiotherapist will show you how and how long. Doing it at the same time each day, such as after school or before bed, helps make it a habit that lasts.
4. **When should we think about a wheelchair?** Many children start using one for long distances, such as school trips or shopping, while still walking at home and in class. Starting early saves energy and keeps your child out with friends. The doctor helps you choose the right chair and plan the change at your child's pace.
5. **What should the school know in an emergency?** Give the school a short written note with your child's diagnosis, medicines, your contact numbers, and the doctor's details. Include that falls may cause fractures, that breathing problems need urgent care, and that steroid medicines should be mentioned to any emergency doctor. Update it every school year.

### In brief

- Most falls in older adults happen at home, often in the bathroom, on stairs or on the way to the toilet at night.
- Small, low-cost changes help: grab rails, non-slip surfaces, firm mats, night lights and well-fitting footwear.
- Home changes work better when combined with strength and balance exercise and a medicine review.
- At Rehabify in Bengaluru, Dr. Dharshan N A, a physiatrist, assesses why a person is unsteady and gives families a home safety plan.

### Why the home matters

About one in three people over 65 falls each year, and many of these falls happen in familiar rooms. A wet bathroom floor, a loose mat, a dark corridor at night or a step without a rail can be enough to cause a fall when legs are weaker or balance is slower.

A fall can lead to a broken hip or wrist, a head injury, or a loss of confidence that keeps a person indoors. The good news is that many hazards are easy to spot and fix. A fresh look at the house, room by room, is a good place to start.

### An Indian-home checklist

Walk through the house with your parent or relative and go through each area together.

**Bathrooms**

- Fix grab rails beside the toilet and in the bathing area. Towel rods and taps are not strong enough to hold body weight.
- Use a non-slip mat or anti-skid tiles where water collects. Wipe the floor dry after bathing.
- A sturdy plastic stool or shower chair lets a person sit while bathing with a bucket and mug, instead of squatting.
- If squatting is hard, a western toilet or a raised commode seat over an Indian toilet makes getting up safer.
- Keep soap, oil and the mug within easy reach so no one bends or stretches on a wet floor.
- Avoid locking the bathroom door from inside, or fit a lock that opens from outside in an emergency.

**Floor mats and floors**

- Remove small loose mats, or choose mats with a rubber backing that grips the floor.
- Watch for curled edges on durries and carpets, and tape or remove them.
- Mop floors when the person is resting, and keep them dry before they walk on them.
- Keep walkways clear of wires, buckets, footwear, bags and pooja items.
- Mark raised door thresholds with a strip of bright tape so they are easy to see.

**Stairs**

- Fit a firm handrail on both sides if possible, running the full length of the stairs.
- Mark the edge of each step with a contrasting strip, especially the top and bottom steps.
- Keep stairs free of clutter, chappals and stored items.
- Make sure there are light switches at both the top and the bottom.
- On outside steps and terraces, watch for moss and wet patches in the monsoon.

**Lighting at night**

- Place night lights or motion-sensor lights between the bed and the bathroom.
- Keep a torch or a light switch within reach of the bed.
- Use brighter bulbs in corridors, stairs and the kitchen, and replace failed bulbs quickly.
- Plan for power cuts with a rechargeable emergency light near the bed.
- Sit on the edge of the bed for a minute before standing at night, to avoid dizziness.

**Footwear**

- Choose closed-back footwear with a thin, firm, non-slip sole that fits well.
- Avoid loose slippers, worn-out chappals, high heels and walking in socks on smooth floors.
- Use separate non-slip footwear for the bathroom.
- Check feet regularly. Pain, swelling, long nails or numbness, common with diabetes, all affect balance.

**Other rooms**

- Keep often-used items in the kitchen and bedroom between waist and shoulder height, so no one climbs on a stool.
- Choose chairs and a bed of a height that makes it easy to stand up, with armrests where possible.
- Keep a phone within reach and save emergency numbers on it.

### Home changes are only one part

Guidelines on falls prevention agree that the home is one piece of a larger picture. The person also needs:

- **Strength and balance exercise,** done regularly over months, with a physiotherapist and at home
- **A medicine review,** because sleeping tablets, some blood pressure medicines and many medicines taken together can cause dizziness
- **Eye checks,** and care when changing to new glasses
- **The right walking aid,** if one is needed, at the right height
- **A check for dizziness on standing,** low blood pressure, foot problems and other health conditions

For people who have already fallen, a home visit by an occupational therapist or a trained professional can find hazards that families miss.

### How Rehabify helps

This includes strength, walking, blood pressure lying and standing, feet, eyesight questions and a review of medicines. Families leave with a written home safety checklist, a home exercise plan and advice on footwear and walking aids. ### When to get help urgently

Callout:

> Call 108 or 112 or go to an emergency department after a fall if there is a head injury and the person takes blood thinners, severe pain, a leg that looks shortened or turned, inability to stand, confusion, or sudden weakness, numbness or slurred speech.

### FAQs

1. **Which room is the most common place for falls?** The bathroom is a common place, because floors are wet and people bend, squat and turn there. The path from the bed to the toilet at night is another risky area. Start your safety changes in these two places first, then move on to stairs, the kitchen and other rooms.
2. **Are grab rails really needed if my parent is still steady?** They are a sensible step for anyone over 65, and easier to fit before a fall than after one. Balance can change quickly after an illness, a new medicine or a hospital stay. A firm rail beside the toilet and in the bathing area costs little and gives support when it is needed.
3. **What footwear is safest at home?** Footwear that fits well, stays on the foot and has a thin, firm, non-slip sole. A closed back is safer than a loose slipper. Walking barefoot on a dry floor may be fine for some people, but socks on smooth tiles or marble are slippery and should be avoided.
4. **Will making the home safer stop all falls?** No single step can prevent every fall. Home changes lower the risk, and they work better when combined with strength and balance exercise, a medicine review, eye checks and the right walking aid. The doctor will look at all of these together and explain what is likely for you.
5. **Can you check our home by video?** In many cases, yes. During a video consult, a family member can walk the camera through the bathroom, bedroom and stairs, and the doctor can point out hazards and suggest changes. A clinic visit is still needed for the balance examination and some tests.

### In brief

- Osteoporosis means thin, weak bones that break easily. It causes no symptoms until a bone breaks.
- Women after menopause and men over 50 with risk factors should ask their doctor about testing, usually a bone density (DEXA) scan.
- The right exercise, enough calcium, protein and vitamin D, medicines your doctor may prescribe, and fall prevention all lower the chance of a fracture.
- At Rehabify in Bengaluru, Dr. Dharshan N A, a physiatrist, assesses bone health and fall risk and plans safe exercise.

### What osteoporosis is

Bone is living tissue that is always being broken down and rebuilt. After about the age of 35, we slowly lose more bone than we build. In women, this loss speeds up for several years after menopause. When bones become thin enough to break after a minor fall, or even a cough or a bend, this is called osteoporosis.

The most common fractures are in the hip, the spine and the wrist. A hip fracture often needs surgery and a long recovery. Spine fractures may cause sudden back pain, or happen quietly and show up only as loss of height or a curved upper back.

### Who should be tested

Talk to your doctor about a bone health check if you are over 50 and have any of these:

- A broken bone after a minor fall from standing height or less
- Loss of height, a stooped back, or new back pain without an injury
- Early menopause, before 45
- Long-term steroid tablets, or some other medicines your doctor will ask about
- A parent who broke a hip
- Low body weight, smoking or heavy alcohol use
- Rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid problems, diabetes, kidney or liver disease, or conditions that affect how food is absorbed

Women over 65 and men over 70 are often checked even without other risk factors.

### What the tests involve

- **A fracture risk assessment.** The doctor uses your age, sex, weight, past fractures and other risk factors to estimate your chance of a fracture over the next 10 years. Tools such as FRAX, developed with the WHO, help with this.
- **A DEXA scan.** A quick, painless scan with a very low dose of radiation that measures bone density, usually at the hip and spine. The result is given as a T-score. A lower score means thinner bone.
- **Blood tests,** such as calcium, vitamin D, kidney and thyroid tests, to look for causes that can be treated.
- **Spine X-rays** if a fracture is suspected.

The doctor uses all of these together, not a single number, to decide whether you need treatment.

### Exercise that helps bones

Bones respond to load. Muscles that pull on bones, and the impact of walking, help keep bones stronger. Exercise also improves balance, which matters just as much, because most hip and wrist fractures follow a fall.

A good programme often includes:

- **Strength training** two or three times a week for the legs, hips, back and arms, using body weight, resistance bands or light weights
- **Weight-bearing activity** such as brisk walking or climbing stairs, as your joints allow
- **Balance training** such as standing on one leg near a support, heel-to-toe walking or tai chi
- **Posture exercises** to strengthen the back muscles and reduce stooping

What to avoid or be careful with:

- Bending forward from the waist with a load, such as lifting a bucket of water or a heavy bag
- Sudden or forceful twisting of the spine
- Some yoga poses with deep forward bends or twists, if you have had a spine fracture. Ask your doctor which poses are safe.
- High-impact jumping, if you have had fractures or have very low bone density

Start gently and build up over weeks. A physiotherapist can teach safe ways to bend, lift and get up from the floor.

### Food, sunlight and medicines

- **Calcium** from milk, curd, paneer, ragi, sesame, green leafy vegetables and pulses. Your doctor will advise if supplements are needed.
- **Vitamin D** from careful sun exposure and, often, supplements. Low vitamin D is common in India even in sunny cities.
- **Protein** at each meal, to support muscles and bones.
- **Stop smoking and limit alcohol.**
- **Medicines your doctor may prescribe** to slow bone loss or help build bone. These are chosen based on your fracture risk, other health conditions and other medicines. Do not start or stop any of them without talking to your doctor.

### Fall prevention is fracture prevention

Strong bones are only part of the story. Lowering the chance of a fall protects the hip, wrist and spine directly:

- Keep up regular strength and balance exercise
- Ask for a review of medicines that cause dizziness or drowsiness
- Make the bathroom, stairs and night-time path safer
- Wear well-fitting, non-slip footwear
- Have eyes checked, and use a walking aid if one is advised

### FAQs

1. **At what age should I get a DEXA scan?** Many guidelines suggest testing women from 65 and men from 70, and earlier for people over 50 with risk factors such as a fracture after a minor fall, early menopause or long-term steroid tablets. Your doctor will advise when a scan is useful and how often it should be repeated.
2. **I have osteoporosis. Can I still walk and exercise?** Yes, and you should. Walking, strength training and balance exercise help both bones and steadiness. Avoid bending forward with a load and sudden twisting. A physiotherapist can show you safe movements and help you build up step by step, especially after a fracture.
3. **Is calcium and vitamin D enough?** For some people with mild bone loss, it may be part of the plan along with exercise. Many people with osteoporosis also need medicines your doctor may prescribe, plus fall prevention. The right mix depends on your fracture risk, which the doctor will explain after your tests.
4. **Can osteoporosis cause back pain?** Osteoporosis itself does not hurt, but a spine fracture can cause sudden back pain, sometimes after a cough, a bend or no clear injury. Loss of height and a curved upper back can also be signs. New back pain over 50 should be checked by a doctor.
5. **Do men need to worry about osteoporosis?** Yes. Men lose bone too, and about one in five men over 50 may have a fracture linked to osteoporosis in their lifetime. Men are tested less often, so it is frequently missed. A fracture after a minor fall in a man over 50 should prompt a bone health check.

### In brief

- A walking aid can make walking safer and help you stay active, but the wrong aid, or one at the wrong height, can raise the risk of a fall.
- A cane suits mild unsteadiness or pain in one leg. A walker suits poorer balance or weakness in both legs.
- A cane is usually held in the hand opposite the weak or painful leg, with the handle at wrist height.

### Do you need a walking aid at all?

Many older adults buy a walking stick from a shop, or use one handed down in the family, without a check. Others avoid one because they feel it makes them look old. Both can be risky.

A walking aid may help if you:

- Hold walls or furniture to move around the house
- Feel unsteady when turning, on uneven ground or in crowds
- Have fallen, or nearly fallen, in the last year
- Have pain or weakness in one leg that makes you limp
- Are recovering from a stroke, a fracture or joint surgery
- Tire quickly and need to stop and rest when walking

You may not need one if you walk steadily, can turn and step over objects safely, and have had no falls. In that case, strength and balance exercise is often more useful than an aid. A walking aid is not a reason to stop exercising. Many people use an aid for a while, build strength and balance, and later need it less.

### The main types

- **Single-point cane (walking stick).** For mild unsteadiness, or pain in one hip or knee. Light and easy to carry.
- **Quad cane.** A cane with four small feet at the base. It stands on its own and gives more support. Often used after a stroke, when only one hand can hold an aid.
- **Walker (frame) without wheels.** Gives much more support. You lift it, move it forward, then step into it. Suits poor balance or weakness in both legs, but walking is slower.
- **Wheeled walker (rollator).** Two or four wheels, so you do not need to lift it. A four-wheeled walker often has brakes and a seat. It suits people who need support but walk at a steadier pace, and needs good hand control for the brakes.
- **Crutches.** Usually used for a short time after an injury or surgery, when weight on one leg must be limited.

### How a cane should be sized

A cane at the wrong height makes you stoop or lifts your shoulder, and can make you less steady.

1. Wear your usual shoes and stand up straight.
2. Let your arms hang loosely at your sides.
3. The top of the cane handle should reach the crease of your wrist.
4. When you hold the handle, your elbow should be slightly bent, about 15 to 30 degrees.

Adjustable canes have a push button to change the height. A wooden stick can be cut to length by a trained person. Check that the rubber tip is in good condition and replace it when worn, because a smooth tip can slip on wet floors. The same height rule applies to the handles of a walker.

### Which hand holds the cane

Hold the cane in the hand **opposite** the weak or painful leg. If your right knee hurts, hold the cane in your left hand.

This matches the natural swing of the arms and takes weight off the painful side. Move the cane forward at the same time as the weak or painful leg, then step through with the stronger leg.

After a stroke, the cane is held in the stronger hand, which is also on the side opposite the weak leg.

### Using stairs with a cane

A simple rule: **up with the good, down with the bad.**

- **Going up:** hold the handrail, step up with the stronger leg first, then bring up the weaker leg and the cane.
- **Going down:** put the cane down first, then the weaker leg, then the stronger leg.

Always use a handrail when there is one. Take one step at a time.

### Walking aids after a stroke

After a stroke, the choice of aid changes as recovery progresses. Early on, a walker or quad cane may give the support needed to walk safely. Later, some people move to a single cane, or walk without an aid. An ankle splint may also help if the foot drags. A physiatrist and physiotherapist review this regularly, so the aid supports recovery and does not hold it back.

### FAQs

1. **Will using a walking stick make my legs weaker?** Not if you keep active. An aid helps you walk more, and walking more keeps legs stronger. Weakness is more likely if fear of falling keeps you at home. Combine the aid with a strength and balance programme, and the doctor will review whether you still need it over time.
2. **Which hand should I hold my cane in?** Usually the hand opposite the weak or painful leg. If your left hip hurts, hold the cane in your right hand, and move it forward with the left leg. After a stroke, it is held in the stronger hand. A physiotherapist can watch you walk and correct the pattern.
3. **How do I know if my cane is the right height?** Stand straight in your usual shoes with arms hanging down. The top of the cane should reach the crease of your wrist. When you hold it, your elbow should bend slightly. If you stoop forward or your shoulder rises, the height needs adjusting.
4. **Should I choose a walker with wheels or without?** It depends on your balance, strength and how you walk. A walker without wheels gives the most stability but is slower. A wheeled walker suits a steadier walking pace and needs good hand control for the brakes. The doctor or therapist will advise after watching you walk.
5. **Can I use a walking aid in the bathroom?** Walkers often do not fit in small Indian bathrooms, and a wet floor makes any aid less safe. Grab rails, a non-slip mat and a shower chair are usually safer there. Ask the doctor or therapist to plan how you move from the walker to the bathroom safely.
